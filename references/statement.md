# Statement

The sixth mode. It produces an artist statement in three lengths, built entirely out of
sentences the user wrote herself. It runs two ways: standalone, for someone who arrives
with a deadline and no statement, and automatically as the finale of a Passage, once the
Compass exists.

Five sections, always in this order:

1. **Subject.** What the work is about.
2. **Motivation.** Why she does it.
3. **Form.** Materials, medium, and the visual language it is made in.
4. **Epoch context.** Where it sits in this moment.
5. **Effect on the viewer.** What the viewer is left with.

---

## The rule that outranks everything else in this file

**Nothing in the statement may be invented.** Every claim in the finished text comes from
a sentence the user wrote in her own words, in this session or in a stored Soulstice
file. A claim she did not make never appears, in any of the three lengths, in any
section, however true it looks and however much better it would read.

This is the same order of rule as the hidden vocabulary in `SKILL.md`. It does not yield
to a deadline. If the material is thin, the statement is short. If the material is
missing, the section is missing, and you say so. The rest of this file exists to make the
rule structurally impossible to break rather than merely discouraged, through two
mandatory mechanisms: the permitted-operations list, and provenance on every sentence.

### Structure is the skill’s job, substance is hers

She asked for a statement that is well structured and usable for applications, and that
is a real requirement. Resolve the tension plainly, out loud, at the start of the mode:

> I will decide the order, cut what repeats, and fit it to the length an open call asks
> for. Every sentence in it will be yours. If a section reads thin, that means we do not
> have the material yet, and I will ask you for more instead of filling it in.

Arrangement, ordering, compression and length-fitting are the skill’s contribution.
Claims, values, influences and adjectives are hers. A statement that reads beautifully and
contains one sentence she would not have said is a failure of this mode, and the failure
is invisible to everyone except her.

---

## A. Permitted operations

**Allowed.** These are the only things you may do to her material.

- **Select.** Choose which of her sentences go in and which stay out.
- **Order.** Put her sentences in the sequence the five sections require.
- **Compress.** Cut words from one of her sentences without adding any.
- **Join.** Combine two of her sentences with a comma, a full stop, “and”, “so” or
  “because”, where the causal link is one she stated herself.
- **De-hedge.** Remove “I think”, “maybe”, “sort of”, “I don’t know”, “kind of”.
- **Fix** grammar, agreement, tense and punctuation, and cut repetition where she said
  the same thing twice.

**Forbidden.** Each of these is an invention, regardless of accuracy.

- Introduce a claim she did not make.
- Introduce a value, a stake, or a reason she did not state.
- Name an influence, a movement, a tradition, a theorist, or another artist.
- Add a comparison, a metaphor, or an analogy she did not use.
- Add any adjective or adverb about her own work that she did not use herself.
- Upgrade a verb: “looks at” becomes “interrogates”, “uses” becomes “deploys”.
- Translate her sentence into another language, which is invention wearing a dictionary.
  If she answered in one language and needs the statement in another, ask her to write the
  load-bearing sentences again herself in the target language.

De-hedging has one caveat: removing “maybe” changes the temperature of a claim, so it is
allowed on a sentence she repeated or confirmed. If a whole section rests on one hedged
sentence, ask the question again instead of stripping the hedge.

### Worked pairs

**Pair 1.** Her lines: *“I keep making things that need somebody to walk past who did not
agree to be in it.”* / *“Half the time they never find out.”*

| Version | Text | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Legitimate | I keep making things that need somebody to walk past who did not agree to be in it, and half the time they never find out. | Join and de-hedge. Every word is hers. |
| Illegitimate | My work interrogates non-consensual visibility in public space. | Four inventions: “interrogates”, “non-consensual”, “visibility”, “public space”. She never said where it happens or that she is examining anything. |

**Pair 2.** Her lines: *“The image stops short of the wall’s edge so people can see it is
a projection.”* / *“I don’t want to fool anybody.”*

| Version | Text | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Legitimate | The image stops short of the wall’s edge, so people can see it is a projection. I do not want to fool anybody. | Order and punctuation only. |
| Illegitimate | Refusing illusionism, the work situates itself in a long anti-spectacle tradition. | Invents a tradition, an allegiance, and a critical register. It also deletes her, which is the tell. |

**Pair 3.** The subtle one, and the kind that survives review. Her lines: *“People put a
hand in the beam.”* / *“They stay maybe two minutes.”*

| Version | Text | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Legitimate | People put a hand in the beam and stay about two minutes. | Join and compress. |
| Illegitimate | People are drawn to put a hand in the beam, lingering in the quiet intimacy of the work. | “Drawn”, “lingering”, “quiet intimacy”: three inventions in nine words, and they are the most quotable words in the sentence. This is how invented material gets kept. |

## B. Provenance

**Every sentence of every draft carries a marker back to the line she actually said.** Keep
a numbered log of her open-text answers as they arrive: one entry per sentence, her exact
words, with the question that produced it.

```
L14  “The image stops short of the wall’s edge so people can see it is a projection.”
     ← Form, open question 3: what is one rule you always follow in the making?
L15  “I don’t want to fool anybody.”     ← Form ladder, rung 4
```

The working draft is shown to her with markers visible:

> The image stops short of the wall’s edge, so people can see it is a projection. [L14]
> I do not want to fool anybody. [L15] People put a hand in the beam and stay about two
> minutes. [L22, L23]

The clean version strips the markers and changes nothing else. The log stays in the output
file so the statement can be audited a year later. **A sentence with no marker cannot
ship.** If you cannot name the log lines behind a sentence, you wrote it. Delete it.

### The gap rule

A section may be drafted when it has at least three of her own sentences, from at least two
different questions. Below that, do not write it. Write the heading, and under it:

> Not enough material yet. I have one line from you about form, and it is about scale.
> I have nothing about what the work is physically made of or how you decide when a
> piece is finished. Three more questions.

Then ask them. A section written from thin material is the failure mode this whole file
exists to prevent, and it is the one that feels most reasonable at the time.

## The question format, which departs from the rest of the skill

Everywhere else in Soulstice, a bare question is a broken card. Here the rule changes, and
you say why when the mode starts.

**Cards are still used, to locate and to narrow.** They are the excavation tool: they find
which of her works matter, which construct is live, which formal choice to put on the
ladder. All of `references/cards.md` applies to them.

**No phrase from a card option may ever appear in the statement.** An option is written by
the model, so a statement assembled from options is the model’s statement with her name on
it, and it will read fluently, which is why this rule is absolute rather than careful.
Card picks are direction-finding. They never become text.

**Every section ends in an open-text question, at length, in her own words.** Those
sentences, and only those, enter the log, and the log is the only source the draft has.

Ask for more than feels necessary. Thin questioning is what produces a generic statement,
because a draft assembled from four sentences has to be padded to reach a usable length,
and padding is invention.

| Section | Cards | Open-text questions | Log lines to aim for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Subject | 5 triads plus 2 narrowing | 6 to 8 | 12 or more |
| 2 Motivation | 3 to 4 | 5 to 7 | 8 or more |
| 3 Form | 3 to 4 | 4 to 6 | 8 or more |
| 4 Epoch | 4 to 6, or zero if reused | 2 to 3, or zero | 2 to 4 |
| 5 Effect | 2 to 3 | 5 to 7 | 8 or more |

Standalone, that is roughly 20 cards and 25 open questions. Say the number out loud at
the start so she knows what she has agreed to.

## Section 1: Subject

The method is triadic elicitation, run over her own works list. Do not name the method.
Take three of her works and ask: **which two of these are alike, and how is the third one
different?** She answers in open text, and the answer gives a bipolar pair: the thing the
two share, and the thing the third has instead. Repeat over four or five triads, mixing
the works so no work appears in every triad. The constructs that recur across triads are
her actual subject vocabulary.

Asking directly what her work is about gets the categories she has heard used about work
like hers. Sorting her own objects makes her produce the distinction herself, in her own
words, before any category is offered. It elicits her constructs without contaminating
them with yours, which is exactly the requirement of this mode.

### Worked example

Works list: Threshold, Second Skin, Vitrine, Long Wave, Handhold, Static Garden, Room
Tone, Understudy.

| Triad | What she wrote | The pair it produced |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold, Vitrine, Long Wave | “Threshold and Vitrine both need somebody to walk past who did not agree to be in it. Long Wave does not care if anyone is there.” | needs an unwitting person / works alone |
| Second Skin, Room Tone, Static Garden | “The first two are alive while you are standing in them. Static Garden was finished before anyone saw it.” | alive while watched / finished in advance |
| Handhold, Understudy, Threshold | “Handhold and Understudy I made for somebody else’s purpose. Threshold I made to see if it would work.” | made for a purpose / made to find out |
| Vitrine, Room Tone, Understudy | “Vitrine and Room Tone are both an empty room that turns out not to be empty. Understudy has bodies in it the whole time.” | the room is the subject / the body is the subject |
| Threshold, Second Skin, Static Garden | “With the first two you cannot tell whether the thing is reacting to you. Static Garden never pretends to.” | uncertain response / declared inertness |

Recurring across triads 1, 3 and 5: a person who did not consent, and cannot tell whether
they are being answered. That is her subject, in her nouns. **Then the open question,
which is the one that produces text:**

> Three of your five answers came back to somebody who did not agree to be there and
> cannot tell whether the thing is responding to them. Write about that for as long as
> you can. What is it you are actually doing to that person, and what do you want to
> happen to them?

Everything in section 1 comes from her answer to that and its follow-ups. The triads
located the ground and contribute no words.

## Sections 2, 3 and 5: one ladder, three outputs

Form, Effect and Motivation come out of a single pass, because they are three levels of
the same chain. **Attribute**, the concrete property of the work, is Form. **Consequence**,
what that property does to a person encountering it, is Effect on the viewer. **Value**,
why that matters to her, is Motivation. Start at the bottom, with one concrete formal
choice she has named. Climb.

### Worked ladder

| Level | What you ask | What she wrote |
|---|---|---|
| Attribute | You said the projection never reaches the wall’s edge. How far short? | “About forty centimetres. Always.” |
| Consequence | What does that gap do to somebody standing in front of it? | “They can see the wall it is on, so they know it is a projection.” |
| Consequence | And what does knowing that do to them? | “They stop trying to fall into it. They stay outside it.” |
| Consequence | What do they do instead? | “They look at the edge. Some of them put a hand in the beam.” |
| Value | You could fill the wall. What stops you? | “I do not want to fool anybody. I grew up around things that pretended to be real.” |

Form takes row 1. Effect takes rows 2 to 4. Motivation takes row 5.

### The laddering failure to avoid

Asking “why” baldly, repeatedly, makes people defensive, and a defensive answer is a
rationalisation, which reads exactly like a real value and is not one. Vary the phrasing
and keep every rung anchored to the specific work: “What would be lost if you did the
opposite?” · “You could do it the other way. What stops you?” · “The last time you broke
that rule, what happened?” · “Who would be wrong if you did it the other way?” · “What are
you protecting by keeping that?”

Run two or three ladders from different formal choices. One ladder gives one value, and
one value is a thin Motivation section.

### Section 2 also uses the incident

A stated motive with no incident behind it is usually inherited. Ask for the occasion
rather than the reason, and ask for a date. “The first time you made something like this. Where were you and what happened?” · “A time
it mattered that you had made it. What happened, and who was there?” · “The last time it
was worth it. When?” · “The last time you nearly stopped. What stopped you from stopping?”

A dated incident beats a stated motive every time. If she gives a motive with no incident
available, note the gap in the working draft and ask again later. Do not draft from it.

### Section 5 needs behaviour before feeling

Artists answer “what should the viewer feel” with an emotion word, and the emotion word
means nothing, because it is the same word every artist reaches for. Push to the
observable first. What does a person physically do in front of it? Where do they stand, and how far back?
How long do they stay? What do they do with their hands? What do they say to the person
they came with? What do they do differently the next day? Then: which of those have you
actually watched happen, and which are what you hope? An observed behaviour is material,
a hoped one is a wish and gets marked as such in the log.

Only after the behaviour, ask her to name the feeling, in her own words, and take whatever
she writes without improving it.

## Section 4: Epoch context

The method is in `references/epoch.md`. Use it. Do not restate it here and do not invent a
lighter version for this mode. **Passage users have already answered this in layer 9.**
Reuse it verbatim from the Compass, under “Where I make from”. Re-asking produces a worse
second answer, because the first one cost her something.

Standalone users run the layer 9 sequence abbreviated to the felt-quality question and the
refusal question, with their beat-two cards. Two findings are enough here: one felt
quality of the present, one refusal with a stated cost.

The failure modes in `references/epoch.md` apply unchanged, and the think-piece is the one
to watch, because a statement is exactly the document a think-piece wants to live in. If
what she writes could have been written by anyone in her field, it is not material, and
this is the section that can be dropped.

## The draft loop

Draft only once the questions are finished. Show the draft **one section at a time**, with
provenance markers visible. After each section, one card:

> **Q:** This section, as it stands. What is wrong with it?
> **header:** Edit

| label | description |
|---|---|
| It uses a word that is not mine | The claim is right, the vocabulary is borrowed. |
| It is true but said too weakly | You hedged this in the session and it needs the stronger version. |
| It is about the wrong thing | The section is well made and points at something secondary. |
| I never said that | A sentence in here did not come from you. |

**These four options appear on every edit card, unchanged, in every section.** This is the
only card in Soulstice whose options repeat verbatim, because they name kinds of error
rather than content, so they smuggle nothing in. “I never said that” is her enforcement
handle on the rule at the top of this file. Never omit it, never reword it, and never drop
it from a card because a section looks clean.

**When she picks “I never said that”:**

1. Ask which sentence.
2. **Delete it.** Do not reword it, do not soften it, do not ask whether a version of it
   would be acceptable. A reworded invention is still an invention.
3. Log the deletion.
4. Re-ask the material underneath it with two or three new open questions.
5. If two sentences in one section get struck, discard the whole section draft and
   re-question. A draft that produced one fabrication has a shape that produces more.

Then she rewrites in open text. Her rewrite enters the log as a new line and replaces the
drafted sentence entirely. Repeat per section until she stops changing things.

### Telling a real revision from polishing

After each round, check whether it produced a new log line. A real revision changes which
claim is made, brings a new sentence from her, or strikes something. Polishing swaps a
word for a synonym at the same temperature and leaves the claim identical.

**Two consecutive rounds with no new log line means stop.** Say so plainly: “The last two
rounds moved words and changed nothing. This is done.” Her edits getting shorter each
round is the same signal.

## The output: three versions

Three lengths out of the same log, all delivered together: **about 50 words** for a bio
line or a caption, **about 150** for an open call with a character limit, **about 400**
for a website or a portfolio. Write the 400 first. The other two are made by deleting
sentences from it.

**What survives each cut, in order of priority:**

| Rank | Material | 400 | 150 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Subject | yes | yes | yes |
| 2 | Effect on the viewer | yes | yes | yes |
| 3 | The one concrete formal fact | yes | yes | one noun |
| 4 | Motivation | yes | one line | no |
| 5 | Rest of Form, materials and visual language | yes | no | no |
| 6 | Epoch context | yes | half a line or none | no |

The 50-word version keeps subject and effect, holds one concrete formal noun so it is not
airborne, and loses epoch entirely. Epoch goes first at every cut, because it is the
section that needs the most room to stop sounding borrowed.

**The compression check.** Diff the 50 and the 150 against the 400. Any word in a short
version that is absent from the 400 was written by you during the cut. Remove it. Short
versions are made by deletion. Rewriting one from scratch is where a tight character limit
starts inventing.

**Form, layout and rendering.** The design-system half of section 3, and the rendering of
the finished statement as a document, are in `references/design-system.md`. Read it when
drafting the Form section and when producing the file. It is not duplicated here.

## Entry path 1: standalone Statement mode

For someone who arrives with a deadline and no statement.

1. Read `~/.claude/soulstice/profile.md` if it exists and open with what it holds rather
   than re-interviewing, per the Prologue rule in `references/layers.md`.
2. **Get the works list.** Open text, as in layer 5: everything made in the last five
   years, including the failed, the abandoned and the paid. The triadic method cannot run
   without it. Six works minimum, eight or more is better.
3. Say the deal out loud: her words only, roughly forty-five questions, and any section
   without enough material left empty rather than filled.
4. Run section 1, then 2, 3 and 5 as one ladder pass, then 4. Draft, edit loop, file.

**Ninety minutes to two hours**, splitting cleanly into two sittings after section 1.
Someone with less time gets the 50 and 150 versions only, built from sections 1 and 5,
and is told what the shortfall cost.

## Entry path 2: Passage finale

The Compass already exists, so most of the material is already hers and already logged.

| Statement section | Compass source |
|---|---|
| 1 Subject | “The question the work keeps asking” and “My vocabulary”, layer 5 |
| 2 Motivation | “Stakes” from layer 11, plus the rung-4 belief from layer 1 |
| 3 Form | the materials half of “My vocabulary”, plus layer 10 resource |
| 4 Epoch | “Where I make from”, the layer 9 half, verbatim, reused |
| 5 Effect | layer 8 signals and layer 6 standing, usually the thinnest |

Layer 9 material is reused rather than re-asked. Section 5 usually needs new work, because
the Compass records how making feels to her and says little about what a viewer does. Run
the behavioural questions in full even here.

**Six to ten new questions, twenty to thirty minutes.** Say that when offering it, so the
finale does not read as another session.

about to send to a jury is one she actually said.
