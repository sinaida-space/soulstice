# Unpacking a global answer

Sooner or later she gives you a label: "fear of failure", "impostor syndrome",
"perfectionism", "I lack discipline", "commercial pressure", "I am not ambitious
enough".

**These are real.** Fear of failure is a genuine fear, and naming it is a correct
answer. Treat it as the front door rather than as a dodge.

What it is not is workable. A label names a class of experiences and cannot be tested,
acted on, or written into a Compass. You cannot run an experiment against "fear of
failure" because there is nothing specific enough to be proved wrong. So the label is
where the inquiry starts, and this file is how to open it.

Everything below runs as cards. The ladder is the method; the cards are the delivery.

---

## 1. The downward arrow

The core technique, out of cognitive therapy (Beck, and Burns' version of it). Take
the label and ask, repeatedly, what it would mean if it were true. Each answer is more
specific than the last. Four or five rungs reach a core belief.

The form is always the same: **"Suppose that happened. What would that mean?"** Never
"why does that scare you", which asks for analysis and gets a theory.

Worked ladder, starting from "fear of failure":

| Rung | Question | A typical answer |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | The label | I am afraid of failing |
| 1 | What would failing actually look like? Not the feeling, the event. | I show the piece and it lands flat |
| 2 | Suppose it lands flat. What happens next? | Nobody invites me to do it again |
| 3 | Suppose nobody invites you. What would that mean? | That the last three years were a hobby I called a career |
| 4 | Suppose that were true. What would it mean about you? | That I left a real profession for something I was never good enough at |

Rung 4 is workable. Rung 0 is not. The Compass gets rung 4.

**In card form.** Each rung is one card. The options are four candidate answers to
that rung, built from what she has already said. She picks or corrects; either way
you have the next rung's raw material.

**When to stop.** Stop when the answer stops changing, when it turns into a statement
about who she is rather than what happens, or when she visibly flinches. Usually four
rungs. Never go past five; past that it becomes an interrogation.

**Do not do this on a fresh wound.** The downward arrow on a real trauma is not
inquiry, it is harm. If the ladder reaches something clinical, stop and say so.

---

## 2. Threat appraisal

Once you have a specific feared event, break it into its three parts. This is standard
appraisal theory (Lazarus and Folkman: primary appraisal of the threat, secondary
appraisal of resources). Fear inflates when any of the three is misjudged, and each
part fails differently, so each needs its own card.

- **Probability.** How likely is it, honestly? Card: four estimates, phrased as
  frequencies rather than percentages ("once in every three shows", "it has already
  happened twice").
- **Cost.** If it happened, what is the actual damage, in months and in money and in
  who stops answering? Card: four consequences at different magnitudes.
- **Coping.** What would she actually do the next morning? Card: four responses, one
  of which should be something she has already survived once.

The finding is usually that the cost is smaller than the dread and the coping is
better than assumed. Do not say this. Let the cards show it and move on.

---

## 3. Fear-setting

Three columns, from Stoic *premeditatio malorum* by way of Tim Ferriss. Use it after
the ladder, on the specific version, never on the label.

1. **Define.** What exactly goes wrong? The rung-3 or rung-4 answer.
2. **Prevent.** What would reduce the chance of it? Card with four concrete actions.
3. **Repair.** If it happened anyway, what would you do? Card with four recoveries.

Then the question that does the work: **"And what is the cost of not attempting it?"**
Card with four costs, framed in the same units, over the same period. Inaction gets
priced as an event, which is the only way it competes with the vividness of the
feared one.

---

## 4. The distress ladder

Adapted from exposure hierarchies. Instead of one big feared thing, produce a graded
list, which turns an unmovable fear into a first step.

Card, multiSelect off, four versions of the same act at increasing exposure:

- send it to one person who already likes your work
- put it in a portfolio nobody will read closely
- show it to the one person whose opinion actually decides it
- put it in a room with strangers and stand next to it

"Which is the first one you could not do?" The rung just below her answer is the test
that goes in the Compass. The point is a first step that is real, not courage.

---

## 5. Decatastrophising

Three cards, in this order, when the feared event has grown to fill the frame:

1. Worst realistic case. Four options, and none of them may be vague.
2. Most likely case. Four options.
3. "Which of these have you already lived through?" Four past events from what she
   has told you.

Card three is the one that matters. It converts an imagined future into a survived
past, and it only works if you use her real history, so it needs material from
layer 2 or layer 5 to exist.

---

## 6. The thought record

The standard CBT column structure (Beck), adapted so each column is its own card
instead of a worksheet she fills in alone. A worksheet gets the respectable answer.
A card, attacked, gets the real one.

The columns, in order:

1. **Situation.** What was actually happening, at episode level. Where, when, who else
   was there.
2. **Automatic thought.** The sentence that ran through her head, verbatim if she can
   get it. Not the feeling, the sentence that caused it.
3. **Feeling and intensity.** Name the feeling, then a number, zero to ten. The number
   is not decoration. It is what gets re-rated at the end, and a change in it is the
   only evidence the record produces.
4. **Evidence for.** What actually supports the automatic thought. Take this
   seriously; do not let the card imply the thought is simply wrong.
5. **Evidence against.** What the thought leaves out or gets wrong. This is usually
   thinner than she expects, which is the point, but the card has to earn that by
   asking honestly, not by loading the options.
6. **Balanced alternative.** Not the positive opposite. A sentence that holds both
   columns of evidence and is still true.
7. **Feeling, re-rated.** Same feeling, same zero-to-ten scale, after the balanced
   alternative. The comparison against the first number is the result.

**Card by card.** Situation and automatic thought can usually run as one card, four
candidate thoughts built from what she has already told you. Evidence for and evidence
against are separate cards; do not merge them, or the against list gets contaminated
by the for list still being in view. The balanced alternative is built from her own
evidence columns, offered as four candidate sentences, none of them curator-ready.
The re-rating is a short scale card, not open text.

**Worked example.**

| Column | Card content |
|---|---|
| Situation | A gallery does not reply to a proposal sent three weeks ago |
| Automatic thought | They read it and it was not good enough to bother answering |
| Feeling, first rating | Shame, 7 out of 10 |
| Evidence for | Two other galleries also did not reply this year |
| Evidence against | The gallery is three people running a public program with no admin staff; one proposal from the same period that did get a reply took five weeks |
| Balanced alternative | Silence from an understaffed gallery is not a verdict on the work |
| Feeling, re-rated | Shame, 3 out of 10 |

The number moving from 7 to 3 is the finding. Do not comment on it. Move on.

**Failure mode.** The evidence-against card comes back thin because the options
offered were thin. If she cannot generate three items, the card was built from your
imagination instead of her history, and it needs material from layer 2 or layer 5 to
work at all.

## 7. The distortion catalogue

A private reference for building better cards. This vocabulary stays with you and
never reaches her. Nine patterns, the standard set (Burns), each with the
artist-specific shape it usually takes.

| Distortion | What it is | Artist-specific form |
|---|---|---|
| Overgeneralisation | One event becomes a permanent rule | One rejected proposal becomes "nobody funds this kind of work" |
| Mind-reading | Assuming what someone thinks without evidence | "The curator thinks it is derivative" from a curator who has said nothing |
| Fortune-telling | Predicting a bad outcome as fact | "This will not land" before it has been shown to anyone |
| All-or-nothing | Only total success or total failure exist | A show that is well attended but not reviewed counts as a failure |
| Discounting the positive | A good result is explained away | "They only liked it because the room was dark and forgiving" |
| Emotional reasoning | The feeling is treated as proof | "I feel like a fraud, so I must be one" |
| Labelling | A global identity replaces a specific event | "I am a decorator", not "I made something decorative this time" |
| Should-statements | A rule about how things ought to be, aimed at herself | "I should have a signature style by now" |
| Personalisation | Taking responsibility for something outside her control | A venue's low turnout becomes evidence of her own failure to matter |

**Hard rule: never name a distortion to the user.** Not in the question, not in the
description, not in a summary at the end of the layer. The catalogue is for you, so
you can see which of these is operating and build the card that exposes it without
naming it. The card does the work; the vocabulary stays in this file.

**Why.** Naming the distortion hands her a category to argue with instead of an
experience to examine. "That sounds like all-or-nothing thinking" invites a debate
about the label and lets her defend the specific instance by attacking the general
term. A card built from the evidence-for and evidence-against columns above gets the
same correction without giving her anything to argue with, because there is no label
in the room to reject.

## 8. Behavioural experiments

The thought record and the ladder produce a belief. A belief is not yet workable until
it is turned into a prediction and tested in the world. This is the difference between
talking about a belief and changing one.

**Turn the belief into a prediction with a number.** "Nobody will book me again" is
not testable. "If I send this to five choreographers I have worked with before, fewer
than one will reply within a month" is. The number matters because it is the only
thing that can be proved wrong; a vague prediction survives any outcome.

Card: take the rung-4 belief and offer four candidate predictions, each with a number
and a timeframe, built from her actual situation. She picks or corrects.

**Design the smallest test.** Not the test that would settle the question for good.
The smallest one that could produce real information without being safe enough to be
meaningless. Card: four candidate experiments at increasing scale, and the choice
question is "which is the smallest one that would actually tell you something."

**Record what happened against what was predicted.** After the experiment runs, a
single card: the number predicted, four candidate actual outcomes, and which one
occurred. Do not let this become a retrospective essay. The comparison is the entire
content.

| Step | Content |
|---|---|
| Belief | Nobody will book me again after a projection that did not land |
| Prediction | If I write to five past collaborators, none will offer new work within six weeks |
| Test | Send five specific messages, individually addressed, within the next two weeks |
| Actual result | Three replied, one offered a date |
| Comparison | The belief predicted zero out of five. Three replied. |

The gap between prediction and result is the update. State it once, plainly, and move
to what test comes next if the gap was small.

## 9. Self-compassion as a working move

The relevant finding is that self-criticism measurably degrades performance on
subsequent tasks, while self-compassion sustains it. This belongs in the toolkit for
the same reason the ladder does: it is instrumental, and it changes what she can
actually produce next.

**The operational form.** Never ask her to be kind to herself; that request produces
performance, not the effect. Ask what she would say to another artist in her exact
position. Card: four candidate responses to a hypothetical peer who just described
her own situation back to her, none of them soft, all of them something she could
actually say out loud to a colleague she respects.

> **Q:** A friend, another artist, tells you exactly what just happened to you, in the
> same words. What do you say back?
> **header:** To a friend

The gap between that answer and what she has been telling herself is the material.
Do not point it out. Ask her to compare the two directly in the next card.

**The standard trap.** This move is easily heard as consolation, and consolation
closes the inquiry: she takes the kind sentence, feels better, and stops looking.
Keep it open by treating the friend's answer as a claim to be tested against the
evidence columns from the thought record, rather than as comfort to be accepted on
its own. Beat two: "Is that actually true of your situation, or is it just what you
say to be kind?" with four options that force her to check the claim against the
evidence, weighing it by what actually happened rather than by how it feels to hear it.

## 10. Guided discovery

Socratic questioning as practised in CBT: a sequence built to let her notice something
herself, never to be told it. The moment you state the conclusion, the layer is over
and what remains is agreement or argument, neither of which is material.

**The four-move sequence.**

1. **A specific question about a specific detail.** Not "do you see a pattern",
   something concrete she can answer from memory.
2. **A second question that surfaces a related detail**, from a different moment,
   without connecting the two for her.
3. **A question that asks her to compare the two details herself.** This is where the
   pattern gets noticed, if it exists, and it gets noticed by her.
4. **A question that asks what this means going forward.** Only after she has made the
   comparison herself. If you ask this before move 3 lands, you have supplied the
   insight and voided the method.

**The rule.** Never ask a question you already know the answer to in order to
demonstrate a point. That is not guided discovery, it is a quiz with a predetermined
correct response, and she will feel the difference even when she cannot name it. Ask
because you do not yet know what the comparison in move 3 will produce. If you already
know, say what you noticed plainly as a card option for her to reject, per layer craft,
rather than walking her through fake discovery of your own conclusion.

---

## 11. Closing the loop

An unpacked fear is worth nothing until it produces one small, specific, dateable
action. Belief revises on violated prediction, and prediction is violated in the world
rather than in conversation.

Take the rung-4 belief and design one test against it, in the Kegan and Lahey sense:
something she can do this month that she would not do if the belief were true, small
enough to be safe and real enough to be informative.

Give it an implementation intention rather than an intention: **when X, I will do Y.**
"I will send it to one choreographer" fails. "On the first Monday after the render is
done, I send it to one choreographer" is a plan with a trigger, and triggers survive
the loss of motivation.

That sentence goes into the Compass under **One test**.

## Where this gets used

- **Layer 1 (Fear)** runs the full ladder. It is the layer's main method.
- **Layer 3 (Role)** uses the downward arrow on "it would be immodest", which is a
  label like any other.
- **Layer 6 (Standing)** uses it on "I do not care about recognition".
- **Layer 7 (Poisons)** uses it on envy: what would it mean about you if they are
  better.
- **Layer 11 (Stakes)** uses fear-setting on the cost of not attempting.

Anywhere a card comes back with an abstraction in "Other", the ladder is the response.
Do not sigh at a label. It is the door.
