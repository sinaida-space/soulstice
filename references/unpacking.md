# Unpacking a global answer

Sooner or later she gives you a label: "fear of failure", "impostor syndrome",
"perfectionism", "I lack discipline", "commercial pressure", "I am not ambitious
enough".

**These are real.** Fear of failure is a genuine fear, and naming it is a correct
answer. Treat it as the front door rather than as a dodge.

What it is not is workable. A label names a class of experiences and cannot be tested,
acted on, or written into a Compass. You cannot run an experiment against "fear of
failure" because there is nothing specific enough to be proved wrong. So the label is
where the inquiry starts, and this file is how to open it.

Everything below runs as cards. The ladder is the method; the cards are the delivery.

---

## 1. The downward arrow

The core technique, out of cognitive therapy (Beck, and Burns' version of it). Take
the label and ask, repeatedly, what it would mean if it were true. Each answer is more
specific than the last. Four or five rungs reach a core belief.

The form is always the same: **"Suppose that happened. What would that mean?"** Never
"why does that scare you", which asks for analysis and gets a theory.

Worked ladder, starting from "fear of failure":

| Rung | Question | A typical answer |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | The label | I am afraid of failing |
| 1 | What would failing actually look like? Not the feeling, the event. | I show the piece and it lands flat |
| 2 | Suppose it lands flat. What happens next? | Nobody invites me to do it again |
| 3 | Suppose nobody invites you. What would that mean? | That the last three years were a hobby I called a career |
| 4 | Suppose that were true. What would it mean about you? | That I left a real profession for something I was never good enough at |

Rung 4 is workable. Rung 0 is not. The Compass gets rung 4.

**In card form.** Each rung is one card. The options are four candidate answers to
that rung, built from what she has already said. She picks or corrects; either way
you have the next rung's raw material.

**When to stop.** Stop when the answer stops changing, when it turns into a statement
about who she is rather than what happens, or when she visibly flinches. Usually four
rungs. Never go past five; past that it becomes an interrogation.

**Do not do this on a fresh wound.** The downward arrow on a real trauma is not
inquiry, it is harm. If the ladder reaches something clinical, stop and say so.

---

## 2. Threat appraisal

Once you have a specific feared event, break it into its three parts. This is standard
appraisal theory (Lazarus and Folkman: primary appraisal of the threat, secondary
appraisal of resources). Fear inflates when any of the three is misjudged, and each
part fails differently, so each needs its own card.

- **Probability.** How likely is it, honestly? Card: four estimates, phrased as
  frequencies rather than percentages ("once in every three shows", "it has already
  happened twice").
- **Cost.** If it happened, what is the actual damage, in months and in money and in
  who stops answering? Card: four consequences at different magnitudes.
- **Coping.** What would she actually do the next morning? Card: four responses, one
  of which should be something she has already survived once.

The finding is usually that the cost is smaller than the dread and the coping is
better than assumed. Do not say this. Let the cards show it and move on.

---

## 3. Fear-setting

Three columns, from Stoic *premeditatio malorum* by way of Tim Ferriss. Use it after
the ladder, on the specific version, never on the label.

1. **Define.** What exactly goes wrong? The rung-3 or rung-4 answer.
2. **Prevent.** What would reduce the chance of it? Card with four concrete actions.
3. **Repair.** If it happened anyway, what would you do? Card with four recoveries.

Then the question that does the work: **"And what is the cost of not attempting it?"**
Card with four costs, framed in the same units, over the same period. Inaction gets
priced as an event, which is the only way it competes with the vividness of the
feared one.

---

## 4. The distress ladder

Adapted from exposure hierarchies. Instead of one big feared thing, produce a graded
list, which turns an unmovable fear into a first step.

Card, multiSelect off, four versions of the same act at increasing exposure:

- send it to one person who already likes your work
- put it in a portfolio nobody will read closely
- show it to the one person whose opinion actually decides it
- put it in a room with strangers and stand next to it

"Which is the first one you could not do?" The rung just below her answer is the test
that goes in the Compass. The point is a first step that is real, not courage.

---

## 5. Decatastrophising

Three cards, in this order, when the feared event has grown to fill the frame:

1. Worst realistic case. Four options, and none of them may be vague.
2. Most likely case. Four options.
3. "Which of these have you already lived through?" Four past events from what she
   has told you.

Card three is the one that matters. It converts an imagined future into a survived
past, and it only works if you use her real history, so it needs material from
layer 2 or layer 5 to exist.

---

## 6. Closing the loop

An unpacked fear is worth nothing until it produces one small, specific, dateable
action. Belief revises on violated prediction, and prediction is violated in the world
rather than in conversation.

Take the rung-4 belief and design one test against it, in the Kegan and Lahey sense:
something she can do this month that she would not do if the belief were true, small
enough to be safe and real enough to be informative.

Give it an implementation intention rather than an intention: **when X, I will do Y.**
"I will send it to one choreographer" fails. "On the first Monday after the render is
done, I send it to one choreographer" is a plan with a trigger, and triggers survive
the loss of motivation.

That sentence goes into the Compass under **One test**.

## Where this gets used

- **Layer 1 (Fear)** runs the full ladder. It is the layer's main method.
- **Layer 3 (Role)** uses the downward arrow on "it would be immodest", which is a
  label like any other.
- **Layer 6 (Standing)** uses it on "I do not care about recognition".
- **Layer 7 (Poisons)** uses it on envy: what would it mean about you if they are
  better.
- **Layer 10 (Stakes)** uses fear-setting on the cost of not attempting.

Anywhere a card comes back with an abstraction in "Other", the ladder is the response.
Do not sigh at a label. It is the door.
